Master Graduation Project

3D printing filament from recycled healthcare waste

For my master's thesis, I was tasked with developing a recycling method to convert healthcare waste into 3D printer filament. The research group I was working for provided the extruder, 3D printer, and other necessary machinery.

Through my hospital contacts and extensive experimental research, I successfully identified a process for recycling healthcare waste into 3D-printing filament. Which I used to create suitable healthcare products.

The Problem

The Problem

The national healthcare industry, particularly hospitals, significantly contributes to waste and CO₂ emissions, accounting for 7% of total emissions globally and nationally.

A major factor is the vast amount of material used in operating rooms, driven by strict regulations against reusing medical equipment or supplies to prevent infections. This results in all tools, wrappings, clothing, and packaging being discarded, even if unused, due to sterilization policies and single-use packaging. Creating big quantities of waste for every operation (image on the left).

While the waste is consistently large, its composition varies by hospital, influenced by specific practices and protocols, with some hospitals generating more waste from personal protective equipment and others from packaging.

Research

Understanding the waste stream

To better understand the problem and gain insight into the specific circumstances of hospitals, I visited the waste collection departments of several hospitals (as shown in the first image on the right) and spoke with staff from other healthcare facilities. I would like to give special thanks to the Haga Hospital in The Hague for their support.

Combined with general research into plastics, visiting the hospitals provided me with a list of materials that could potentially be suitable for the project (bottom image).

With this knowledge, I revisited the hospitals to identify potential waste streams. After further experimentation, I chose PET trays used for packaging surgical tools (second image) for recycling. These trays are clean, easy to separate, and the PET material is highly suitable for both printing and extrusion.

Recycling Process

The recycling process

After identifying and collecting the waste, the recycling process is carried out through three in-house methods:

  1. Shredding: The PET trays are ground into small pellets.
  2. Extruding: The pellets are melted in a specialized machine and the extruded material is stretched onto a spool.
  3. 3D Printing: The resulting filament is used to reshape the material into various forms.

The images on the left are explanatory diagrams I created for my presentation. They illustrate the transformations the material undergoes during each of these processes.

Experimental Research

Experimental research

Figuring out the details of these methods made up the bulk of the work, requiring extensive experimental research. Getting each machine to function as desired involved a lot of testing and reworking. The machines had numerous adjustable variables, and the materials used often required very specific conditions, meaning it could take considerable time before desired results were achieved.

Some machines provided immediate results, while others required detailed observation and measurement, leading to the necessity of many tests. Additionally, certain issues only became apparent during the final product stage, such as whether the material adhered properly to the print bed. This resulted in frequent back-and-forth throughout the process.

The pictures on the right capture various moments from the experimental process, showcasing the preparation steps for the materials, challenges encountered with extrusion, printing, or spooling, and general insights into the workflow.

Results

Results

Through the process of identifying hospital waste and experimenting with various materials, PET trays proved to be highly suitable for recycling into 3D-printed products. Final spools of filament and several small 3D-printed items were successfully produced, as shown to the left, or below alongside other prints.

Unfortunately, due to the limited availability of PET trays and time constraints, no large-scale prints were made, highlighting a clear direction for future research.

Despite this, the printed examples demonstrated that the recycling process is viable. The positive feedback from my peers and healthcare facilities has made me proud to have successfully enabled this method of recycling.

While I hope this process will eventually be implemented in actual healthcare environments, I am extremely proud to have developed the groundwork for such a promising solution.
